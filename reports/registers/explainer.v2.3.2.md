I purpose, in return for the honour you do me in coming to see what we are about, to bring before you in the course of these lectures the chemical history of a candle. I regard the lectures as a courtesy which I owe to those who come, and I have no thought of making a display for them.

I have taken this subject on a former occasion; and if it were left to my own will, I should prefer to repeat it almost every year, so abundant is the interest which attaches itself to it. The candle is by no means a narrow topic. It opens a way into many different departments of philosophy; and indeed there is not a law under which any part of this universe is governed which does not come into play in the phenomena of a candle.

There is no better and no more open door by which a man may enter into the study of natural philosophy than by considering the physical phenomena of a candle. The beginner needs no special apparatus to make a start; he has only to give his attention to the thing which burns before him. I trust, therefore, that I shall not disappoint you in choosing this for my subject rather than any newer topic. A newer topic could not be better than the candle, and I think it probable that none would be so good.

And before proceeding, let me say this also: that though our subject be so great, and our intention that of treating it honestly, seriously, and philosophically, yet I mean to pass away from all those who are seniors amongst us. I claim the privilege of speaking to juveniles as a juvenile myself, and I have done so on former occasions. Though I stand here with the knowledge that my words will be given to the world, that knowledge shall not deter me from speaking in the same familiar way to those whom I esteem nearest to me on this occasion.

There are some candles which are great curiosities, and there are certain kinds of trees which have become famous for the manner in which they burn. Of these I may take as an example a substance called candle-wood, which is got from some of the bogs in Ireland.

The candle-wood is a hard, strong, excellent wood, evidently fitted for good work as a resister of force, and yet withal burning so well that where it is found the people make splinters of it, and torches, since it burns like a candle and gives a very good light indeed. In this wood we have one of the most beautiful illustrations of the general nature of a candle that I can possibly give.

Its fitness as an illustration lies in the functions which it performs. In the first place, it furnishes fuel. In the next place, it provides a means of bringing that fuel to the place of chemical action; and further, it provides a regular and gradual supply of air to that same place. Lastly, it gives out heat and light. All of these offices are united in one material, so that the candle-wood is in effect a natural candle. We may hold it in mind as the pattern against which to measure the manufactured candles we are now to consider.

The first of these is the candle commonly called a dip. To make dips, lengths of cotton are hung up by a loop and dipped into melted tallow. Each length is taken out and cooled, and then dipped again; and the dipping and cooling are repeated, over and over, until there is an accumulation of tallow round the cotton.

The miners in coal mines used candles of very small size. In former times the miner had to find his own candles, and it was supposed that a small candle would not so soon set fire to the fire-damp in the coal mines as a large one; hence the miner chose small candles, partly for safety and partly for economy, and he had them made at 20, 30, 40, or 60 to the pound. In time their place was taken by the steel-mill, and the steel-mill in its turn gave way to the Davy lamp and the other safety lamps of various kinds.

Here is a candle which Colonel Pasley is said to have taken out of the Royal George. It was sunk in the sea, and lay there exposed for many years to the action of salt water. The specimen shows how well candles may be preserved through long periods. Though it is cracked about and broken a good deal, it burns regularly when it is lighted, and the tallow resumes its natural condition as soon as it is fused.

For many of the illustrations of the candle, and of the materials of which it is made, I am indebted to Mr. Field, of Lambeth.

The first of these materials is suet, the fat of the ox; and Russian tallow, I believe, is employed in the manufacture of dips. A great step was made when Gay-Lussac, or some one who entrusted him with his knowledge, converted that tallow into the beautiful substance called stearin. The difference shows itself at once in use. A modern candle is clean, whereas the ordinary tallow candle is greasy; and the drops which fall from the modern candle may be scraped off and beaten into powder without soiling anything.

The conversion is carried out by several successive operations.

The tallow is first boiled with quicklime, and a soap is made of it. The soap is then decomposed by sulphuric acid, which takes away the lime and leaves the fat rearranged as stearic acid; and at the same time a quantity of glycerin is produced. This glycerin is a sugar, or a substance very like sugar, and it comes out of the tallow in the course of the chemical change. The oil is next pressed out of the stearic acid, and as the pressure increases, the oily part carries the impurities away with it. What remains after the pressing is melted and cast into candles.

The products of this process are the stearin candles, made of stearin which has been obtained from the tallow.

There remain the other materials. The sperm candle comes from the purified oil of the spermaceti whale. Candles are also made of yellow beeswax, and of refined beeswax. Paraffin is another curious substance from which candles are made, and it may be obtained from the bogs of Ireland. Lastly, there is a kind of wax brought from Japan, a new material for the candle-maker, which came within our reach only after Japan had been forced open to the access of other nations.
