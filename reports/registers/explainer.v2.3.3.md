The coastline of the British Isles is not a fixed thing. Its outline changes continually, and in some places the change goes on fast enough that a man may watch it within his own lifetime.

Let us take first the east coast of England, where on certain stretches the sea appears to be slowly creeping in upon the land. Between Harwich and Walton-on-the-Naze there are places which twenty or thirty years ago were grazing-fields, and which are now covered by the sea at every high tide. When the tide goes out they do not return to pasture. At low water they lie as sandy wastes, threaded here and there by little rivulets of sea-water; and the cattle which once grazed upon them find nothing there to graze.

The south coast of the Isle of Wight is losing land in the same way, and it is not alone in this. Near the edge of the cliff between Sandown and the Culver Cliff (which is the most easterly point of the island) there once stood a fort. Within living memory it was possible to climb round the seaward wall of that fort; it is possible no longer, for the outer sea-wall slipped into the sea long ago. Some of the inner fortifications followed it. In the end the whole fort had to be taken down, lest the inner landward wall should slide down the cliff together with the ground on which it stood.

The cause is easily stated. Wind and waves are undermining and honeycombing the cliff, and as they weaken its base and its body, the upper crust, upon which the fort was built, loses its support and slips away. The top of the cliff gives way because the parts beneath it have been worn out. Were nothing done to protect it, the cliff would be gradually eaten away, and its gravel and clay drawn down into the sea. Left to nature, the Isle of Wight would become a little smaller.

Nor are these isolated cases. The same loss of land is going on at many points along the coast of the British Isles; it is going on beyond them also, along the coast of Florida and in the Gulf of California. Heligoland, a small islet in the North Sea, belonged once to Great Britain and was afterwards handed over to Germany. The sea attacks it so fiercely that it must almost be armour-plated to keep it whole, and it is fenced about with stone for its protection.

Erosion of the kind we have seen on the coasts of England and of Heligoland is at work all over the world, and it is nothing new. It has always gone on. Indeed, had there been no coastal erosion in past ages, there would be no British Isles at all.

For England, Scotland and Ireland were once joined to Europe. There was a time when a man might have walked from Harwich to the Hook of Holland across what is now the North Sea, because the North Sea was then dry land. The sea encroached upon that land from the north. From the south the Atlantic Ocean broke through, until the English Channel was bored clean through into the shallow waters of the newly formed North Sea. By this double advance of the sea, lands which had been part of Europe were cut off and became the British Isles.

But the sea does not only take land away; it also gives land back. Sir Thomas Holditch tells us that on parts of the Pacific coast of America there is dry land which once lay beneath the sea, as the shells found upon it plainly show. He tells us further that close beside this raised land a boat may be sailed over forests now sunk beneath the waves. Within a short distance of one another, land has risen out of the sea and land has sunk into it.

Still, the loss of parts of England to the sea is a serious matter. The process by which the sea wears away the land is called sea erosion, and a Royal Commission on Sea Erosion was appointed to inquire how much land had been lost and how the loss might be remedied. How much has England lost? The answer cannot be had by a simple reckoning of losses, for on some parts of the English coast the land is gaining.

The reason is that the material which the sea removes does not vanish. The sand, gravel, chalk, shale and clay which it takes from the cliffs are not lost; wherever the tides and currents lay them down, the sea grows shallower. The sea may spread this material in beds or heap it into sand-banks; or it may carry it round the coast and drop it at some other place. Thus the same sea may tear down the coast at one point while it builds it up at others, and it may even raise natural breakwaters out of material it has carried away from elsewhere.

Rivers, like the sea, lay down beds of material, and their supply begins with the rain. Every shower washes some sand, gravel, loam or chalk from the land into the nearest rivulet. The rivulets carry it to the rivers; the rivers carry it to the sea.

How much sediment a river carries depends upon the speed at which it moves. A swift river carries most of its sediment along with it; this is why rivers are generally muddy after rain, when they are full and running fast. As a river draws near the sea and meets the tides it usually slows down, and when it slows down it drops its sediment. In this way mud-banks or sand-banks are formed at the mouths of rivers.

Suppose a river to be left alone long enough. The sand-banks at its mouth will grow, and the mouth will become shallower and spread; river mouths, in short, silt up with time. A river used by ships cannot be left to do this. Large sums must be spent in dredging away the mud to keep the channels clear, and such dredging is carried on in the Scheldt and at the mouth of the Thames.
