The coastline of the British Isles is not a fixed thing. Its outline is continually changing, and in some places the change is great enough that you may see it with your own eyes within a single lifetime.

On parts of the east coast of England the sea appears to be slowly encroaching upon the land. Between Harwich and Walton-on-the-Naze there are places which twenty or thirty years ago were grazing-fields, and which are now covered by the sea at high tide. Nor do they return to pasture when the tide goes out; at low water they are sandy wastes, threaded by rivulets of sea-water, and the cattle which once grazed upon them find nothing there to graze.

Other coasts are losing land as well, and among them the south coast of the Isle of Wight. Near the edge of the cliff between Sandown and the Culver Cliff (the most easterly point of the island) there once stood a fort. It was formerly possible to climb round its seaward wall; that can no longer be done, for the outer sea-wall slipped into the sea long ago, and some of the inner fortifications went the same way. In the end the whole fort had to be dismantled, lest the inner landward wall should slide down the cliff together with the ground beneath it.

The cause is simple enough. The cliff is made of gravel and clay, and wind and waves are undermining and honeycombing it. As they weaken its base and its body, the upper crust, on which the fort was built, loses its support and slips away; the top of the cliff gives way because the parts beneath it have been worn out. Let us suppose that nothing were done to protect it. The cliff would then be gradually eaten away, and its gravel and clay drawn into the sea; and the Isle of Wight, left to nature, would become a little smaller.

Nor are these isolated cases. The same loss of land is going on at many places along the coast of the British Isles, and it is going on elsewhere also, along the coast of Florida and in the Gulf of California. Heligoland, a small islet in the North Sea which once belonged to Great Britain and was afterwards handed over to Germany, is attacked by the sea so fiercely that it must almost be armour-plated to keep it intact; it is fenced in with stone for its protection.

Erosion of the kind we have seen on the coasts of England and of Heligoland is going on all over the world, and it is nothing new. It has always happened. Indeed, had there been no coastal erosion in past ages, there would be no British Isles at all. England, Scotland and Ireland were once joined to Europe, and the North Sea was dry land; a man might then have walked from Harwich to the Hook of Holland. The sea encroached upon that land from the north. From the south the Atlantic Ocean broke through, until the English Channel was bored through into the shallow waters of the newly formed North Sea; and in this way lands which had been part of Europe became the British Isles.

But the sea does not only take land away; it also gives land back. Sir Thomas Holditch tells us that on parts of the Pacific coast of America there is dry land which once lay beneath the sea, as the shells found upon it show. He tells us further that close beside this raised land a boat may be sailed over forests now sunk beneath the waves. Within a short distance of each other, land has there risen out of the sea and land has sunk into it.

The loss of parts of England to the sea is, for all that, a serious matter. The process by which the sea wears away the land is called sea erosion, and a Royal Commission on Sea Erosion was appointed to inquire how much land had been lost, and how the loss might be remedied. How much has England lost? The answer cannot be given as a simple tally of losses, because in some parts of the English coast the land is gaining.

The reason is that the material which the sea removes does not disappear. The sand, gravel, chalk, shale and clay which it takes from the cliffs are not lost; wherever the tides and currents deposit them, the sea becomes shallower. The sea may lay this material down in beds or sand-banks, or it may carry it round the coast and drop it elsewhere. Thus the same sea may tear down the coast at one point while it builds it up at others; it may even build natural breakwaters out of material which it has taken from some other place.

Rivers, like the sea, lay down beds of material, and their supply begins with the rain. Every shower washes some sand, gravel, loam or chalk from the land into the nearest rivulet; the rivulets carry it to the rivers, and the rivers carry it to the sea. How much sediment a river carries depends upon the speed at which it moves. A swift river carries most of its sediment along with it, and this is why rivers are generally muddy after rain, when they are full and running fast. As a river nears the sea and meets the tides it usually slows down, and when it slows down it drops its sediment. In this way mud-banks or sand-banks are formed at the mouths of rivers.

If a river is left alone long enough, the sand-banks at its mouth will grow until the mouth becomes shallower and spreads; river mouths, therefore, silt up in the course of time. A river used by ships cannot be left to do this. Large sums must be spent in dredging the mud to keep the channels clear, and such dredging is carried on in the Scheldt and at the mouth of the Thames.
